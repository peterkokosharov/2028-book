# Emil, year 2

The first time Emil said "alignment" in a meeting, he drew the quotation marks in the air with two fingers of each
hand, and the room laughed, because the room had been sitting through alignment for years and was glad somebody new
enough to mock it had arrived.

He said "stakeholders" the same way, as if it were the name of a band he did not like. He said "capacity" with an
eyebrow. He said "let's take that offline" in the voice of a newsreader announcing a minor disaster, and the two people
who could read the warehouse code in their sleep grinned at each other across the table.

Three months later he said "alignment" without the fingers. Nobody laughed, or noticed that there was anything to
laugh at, including Emil, who was by then saying it about eleven times a day and would have needed a great deal of
finger.

The words were useful. When the platform team and product wanted
opposite things, "we need alignment" got two senior people into a room faster than "you two are both wrong in
different ways", which was the truth and which he had once written, in his first month as tech lead, in a ticket. That ticket had
taken three weeks and a mediation to recover from. "Alignment" took a calendar invite.

The company had grown. A larger company had bought it in the winter and announced, in a video with a lot of drone
footage of warehouses, that nothing would change. Things changed. There were now four floors, a second coffee machine
with no opinions at all, a reorganisation in March and another in September that undid most of the first one. Emil's
team got a new name in each, and the same work.

In the autumn, a carrier adapter started to drop messages again, one in every few thousand. There was an incident call.
Twelve people, eight cameras off, a shared screen with a graph shaped like a tired staircase.

"Can someone explain how the retry in that adapter actually works?" Emil said. "In detail. I want to understand what
we're dealing with before we go to stakeholders."

There was a pause. Then one of the two people who could read the code in their sleep, the one who wrote "stuff",
shared his screen, opened the file, and ran a command that shows who last changed each line.

Every line of the retry had Emil's name beside it. The date was nearly two years earlier. It was the ticket from his
first nine days, the one Ruth had forwarded to everyone.

"You wrote it," said the man who wrote "stuff". He did not stop sharing his screen.

"I know," said Emil. "That's why I'm asking."

Everybody laughed, cameras on and off. It was the best line of the call; people repeated it at the coffee machine for a
week. Emil laughed too. Then he spent the evening reading his own code as if it were a stranger's, which it now was,
and he found the bug in it at half past eleven, and it was a small one, and it was his.

He did not tell anyone that he had found it himself. He wrote it up as a ticket, beautifully, and gave it to the man
who wrote "stuff", who fixed it in twenty minutes and wrote "stuff" in the commit message.

The kiko macOS section had been fixed in the meantime, by somebody else. Emil saw it in a notification he did not
open, in a mailbox folder called "Other".
