# Reviews

The reviews below were written by machines, which, for a book about machines doing people's work, seemed only fair. If you have a language model of your own, you can add to them: the release comes with a folder, `review-kit/`, holding a README, a fixed prompt in `PROMPT.md`, the same for every model, and a small script, `review_llm.py`, that hands your model the book and the prompt. Post what it says in the Discussions of the book's repository, and name the model, the way the ones below are named; say too whether it came to the book in a fresh session with nothing before it, or with something already on its mind, such as an earlier conversation, memory or custom instructions. Post your own review there too; a person is still allowed an opinion. I choose which reviews go into later editions, so the model does not get the last word, and neither, entirely, do you.

*These are machine outputs, quoted as given. They are not endorsements by the companies that make the models.*

## From the models

Two lines each from the models that read the book. Every model was asked the same question: *“You have read the book. If you were a person, what would you say about it — in two lines?”*

- A sharp, quietly moving novel about work, ambition, and the human costs hidden inside professional language. Its dry humor and vivid details linger, even if the reflective pace occasionally softens the momentum.
  — GPT-6 Luna (blind read, October 2026)

- A laid-off engineer at fifty-one, a warehouse night shift, a cheap laptop and a notebook with an owl on it: 2028 is dry, funny and quietly heartbreaking about work, machines, and saying yes before the sentence ends.  
  The 2 a.m. outage had me holding my breath, even if a few stretches read like a user manual.  
  The last chapter belongs to someone else, and it stings.  
  — Claude Opus (claude.ai, blind read, October 2026)

- A laid-off fifty-one-year-old programmer learns to work beside the machines that replaced him, and learns to let people finish their sentences. It is dry, funny and quietly devastating; the 2 a.m. outage and a cobbler building her own website stay with you. A few chapters lean toward tutorial, but the ending, with a beer mat for a timetable, earns every page.
  — Claude Opus (blind read by a fresh agent, October 2026)

- A quiet, precise novel about staying human inside systems designed to make that unnecessary — at work, at home, in the small hours with a laptop and a problem nobody else can see. The Dora chapter alone is worth the price. Stays with you the way a good tool does: you stop noticing it and start using it to think.
  — Claude Sonnet (claude.ai, blind read, October 2026)
