# Chapter 1: A Laptop Sold by Weight

The letter that ended my life in software was four paragraphs long, and three of them were about how much the
company valued me.

I kept it. It was a well-made object. Somebody had sanded it, run it past a lawyer and past a person whose title
contained the word "people", which in my experience is the word a company uses for the department that removes them.
The first paragraph thanked me. The second praised my "dedication over many years", the phrase you use when you cannot
remember a single thing a man actually did. The third explained that the organisation was "evolving its delivery
model". The fourth, the only one with a verb that did any work, said my role had been made redundant.

I had built the delivery model. The spring before, someone upstairs had asked whether the coding assistants everybody
was talking about could be wired into our build pipeline. I said yes. I wired them in. I wrote the guidelines and the
little script that labelled machine-written pull requests so a human would look twice. I was proud of that script; it was the only thing I enjoyed writing that year. By autumn the team was half its size and the script was
still running. It probably runs today. It does not know I am gone.

Since the spring everyone went home, my office had been the kitchen table, and my day began with a call at nine,
cameras off. For four years the work grew duller as the frameworks grew cleverer. It had come down to clicking
buttons in the right order. A dog would have done it better than me, and I did not have a dog. If I did a task, fine;
if I did not, also fine.

Once or twice a month they called us into the office, to be walked past clients and investors like something rare in
an enclosure, proof that the company had volume; sometimes there was a hotel, and a buffet the length of a corridor. Before the food, we paid for it: a manager told us we were the best of the best, that the company had a future, that we were winning. I had heard the same speech at every job I ever had, including the ones that later thanked me for my dedication. We sat and nodded with our bodies; the rest of us was already at the prawns.
One morning a colleague from the database team, camera on for once, lifted his mug to the screen and said, "Some drink
from sorrow, others from joy — we drink from the morning." Nobody asked what was in the mug. Early in the morning, before
the call, the bottle in the cupboard looked a slightly better decision than it had the day before. I noted that, and
made coffee. In the third winter Helen moved the bottle behind the flour on the top
shelf. She said nothing about it, and neither did I.

Then the letter came. I read it twice and waited for the blow. Something else came instead, and it took me a week to
name it: I had been let off. I slept nine hours. Helen read the letter at the table and said, "Good," and meant the job,
not the money.

The bottle stayed behind the flour. The next May I took the flour down for pancakes, and put it back in front of the bottle.

## The months

The relief lasted until the first bank statement. Everyone who has been through it will tell you the same things, and they are all true and none of them help. You
update the CV. You do a take-home test that is graded by an automated tool, and the tool rejects you in four minutes, for a
reason it does not give.

In the third month I had a real interview, the only one that year with a human being on the other side of the table.
Two rounds. In the first, a technical lead drew a queue on a whiteboard that lost messages under load and asked me why.
I told him why, and then showed him a second bug in his drawing that he had not put there on purpose. He laughed and
wrote something down. I went home and allowed myself a glass of wine I could not quite afford.

The second round was with the head of the department and a woman from recruitment. They had my CV printed out. The
head of the department had circled one thing on it in blue ink, and it was not a job. It was the year I finished
university.

He asked how I would feel in a team where everyone was "at an earlier stage of their career". He asked whether I would
be comfortable taking direction from people with less experience than me. He asked, kindly, whether I still enjoyed
learning new things. The woman from recruitment asked about my energy levels. I had not known that energy came in
levels. I said mine were fine. I was fifty-one, I had slept four hours, and I had found a bug in their test.

The answer came two days later. They had decided to go with a candidate "whose profile better fits the team's current
stage". The advertisement went back up the same afternoon, word for word, with one change. Where it had asked for ten
or more years of experience, it now asked for three to five.

I printed that too. It is in the same folder as the letter. Nobody in that room had said a sentence that could be held
against them, and every one of them knew what they were doing, and so did I, and I answered politely about my energy,
because I needed the job more than I needed to be right. That is the part I have not forgiven. Not them. The politeness.

In the fifth month a job site sent me a suggestion, chosen for me by a matching engine. It was my old job. Same
company, same team; I recognised the names of the queues in the description. The title now had the words
"AI-augmented" in front of it. The salary was lower by roughly the amount I had been paying for
the car. The engine thought I was a strong match. It was right. I had written half the systems in the description and
all of the script that had made me unnecessary.

I did not apply. I am not that strong.

Money runs out at a speed you can calculate, and I calculated it, because that is what I do. I put it in a
spreadsheet. The spreadsheet had a column called "months left", and every month the number in it went down by slightly
more than one.

The car went in March. I sold the good chair, the second monitor, most of the books. The chair and the monitor had
served a company laptop, and the laptop had gone back with my badge; the car had known one road, to the office twice a month, and there was
nothing at the end of it any more. I kept the Douglas Adams paperbacks, because nobody would have paid for them, the spines being held together by habit, and because I had read
them at every bad moment of my life and saw no reason to stop now.

## The kitchen

Through the winter Helen did the crossword at the kitchen table that had been my office for four years, in the evenings, in pencil, while I sat at the other
end of it with the old laptop.

The marriage went in April, though it had been leaving for longer than that.

It went on a weekday morning, over coffee. Helen had made two cups, which she had stopped doing in the winter. She sat down opposite me and turned her cup by the handle, a quarter turn
at a time. She did that when she was counting something.

"Did you know I'd looked at your spreadsheet?" she said.

I had not known that she knew where it was. I kept it in a folder with a dull name, like a man hiding a bottle.

"You've worked it out to the week, haven't you? When the money stops." A quarter turn. "Where's the line for anything
else?"

I said that was not what the spreadsheet was for.

"Then what is?" She looked out at the yard, where the neighbour's washing had been hanging since Sunday. "My sister has
a room. If I went to her, there'd be one less of us in the column, wouldn't there? Would it be all right if I
went —"

"Yes," I said.

She stopped. I had said it before she finished the sentence. I say yes to things.

"I was going to say: for a while, or for good," she said. "I was going to ask you which."

I opened my mouth and found that I had already answered, and that the answer had been to a different question. She
waited. She is a patient woman, more patient than I am, and she waited longer than I would have. Then she nodded,
finished her coffee, washed her cup, dried it, and went to pack. She took the cup with her. It was hers; her mother had
given it to her.

Nobody raised their voice. Two people being very polite in a kitchen, and then one person.
She went for a while, and the while did not end.

## The warehouse

So I took a job in a warehouse. Nights, scanning, on the edge of the city where the buses stop at eleven and start
again at five. The first payslip said that an hour on the scanner paid what I had once been paid for about nine
minutes. I did that arithmetic once. On the phone on Sundays I told my mother that I was doing
some consulting in logistics. She was pleased. I have never corrected it.

The night I remember was in June. A lorry had come in with a leaking roof, the labels on its cartons had gone soft,
and the scanner buzzed at every third one. Every buzz meant typing a fourteen-digit code with my thumb on
a rubber keypad while the belt kept moving. By the door there was a screen with our names and our rates per hour. Mine
was orange, then red, near the bottom. My back had started filing its complaints at midnight and by two it had moved on
to appeals.

At twenty to three a pallet came through of forty-eight laptops, boxed, new, addressed to the building where I used
to work. The city is not large. The same street, the same number, the loading bay where I had parked every day for twelve years, until the spring everyone went home. I scanned them. All forty-eight beeped. Somewhere on that floor, the following week, someone would open one of them
and sit down to do something like my job, and I hoped, in a way that did me no credit, that the script would give
them trouble.

Next line over was John. Big hands, neck like a bollard. He scanned faster than me and talked less. At the break he tore
his sandwich in half and gave me one half. Didn't ask. Then he asked what I used to do. I told him. He chewed.

"The 94," he said. "Twenty-one years. Depot out past the cemetery to the villages and back, six-ten first, eleven-forty last. Now it's every
forty minutes and they need four men, not eleven. New timetable. Somebody made it on a computer." He chewed some more.
"Yours, at least, was something clever."

I could not decide, then or since, whether that was meant as comfort.

Every night the list was new. Every morning it was gone and another would come, longer. I had four hundred more
cartons, and the belt did not wait for me to finish thinking about any of it.

## A job, with a condition

A regional freight forwarder's lorries came to our gate most nights, and most nights their drivers waited, because
their bookings arrived late or not at all. They stood by the cabs smoking and swearing at a system none of them had
seen. I listened for three weeks. Then I wrote one page to their operations manager: what I thought was going wrong
with their messages, and what I would look at first. I took it there myself at seven on a Monday morning, after a
shift, in the clothes I had scanned in, and gave it to the woman at reception, who held it by one corner.

Three weeks later a woman called Hanne rang me. She had the calm of someone who had been lied to by software vendors so
often that she now found it restful.

"You are the person who wrote the letter," she said, when I sat down in her office. "Half of it was correct. I would
like to know whether you can find the other half." She asked three questions about message queues, one about SQL,
and put her pen down. "There is a condition. The board has asked us to adopt AI tools. That is the phrase they use; I
did not choose it. I need people who will work with these tools and tell me honestly what they do, including when the
answer is not the one the board wants. Three months' probation. Can you do that?"

I said yes before she had finished the sentence. It had cost me a kitchen. But I had nothing to
negotiate with except a spreadsheet with a sad column.

On the bus home I took the yes apart. The company would give me an account with one AI vendor; they pay, they choose.
But a supplier had once changed its licence terms on a Tuesday, and by Friday a year of our planning was a historical
document. I did not want my new trade to belong to one company's pricing page. If I was going to learn this, as I once
learned the JVM by breaking it on purpose, I wanted a workshop of my own: a place where I held the keys, could swap one
model for another, and was not billed by the minute while I was being stupid, which is what learning looks like from
the outside.

That is the whole of my reason. It is not ideology. I sold my ideology with the car; it went for less.

On the first morning they gave me a badge with a photograph taken against a wall the colour of old milk, a desk by a
window that did not open, and a company laptop with the vendor's tool already installed and an account already made in
my name. Somebody had typed my name into a form before I arrived. I sat and looked at it for a while. It is a small
thing, to be expected somewhere. I had not been expected anywhere for eleven months.

Then I opened the code. The integration layer had been written by a contractor who had since moved to another country,
another industry and, judging by his last commit message, another state of mind. The first file had a comment at the
top that said, in capitals, DO NOT TOUCH, and underneath, in lower case, a year later, by the same hand, "touched it,
sorry". I read that file to the end before I opened any other.

## The laptop

There is a kind of seller who buys up old office laptops by the pallet and sells them, to people like me, by the piece.
Mine had a lock-up behind a tyre shop. His laptops were stacked like roof tiles, and he priced them by weight and condition with the air of a fishmonger: this one is fresh,
this one you cook tonight.

I chose a business laptop of the kind companies bought by the thousand ten years ago: black, square, with a keyboard
designed by someone who believed in typing. It had a scratch across the lid shaped like a river on a map. It had 8 GB of
memory, a small solid-state disk somebody had fitted later, and a battery that lasted forty minutes if you did not
frighten it. It cost me less than a pair of decent shoes, or, in the only currency I counted in that year, eleven days
of the column. I paid in cash. He put it in a supermarket bag.

The models do not run on a laptop like that. The thinking happens in a data centre, on hardware that costs more than
the street I live on. What runs on the laptop is the conversation, and that needs only a little memory and a network
connection. I put Q4OS on it, a Debian-based Linux with the Trinity desktop: a panel, a menu, windows with borders you
can grab.

## The workshop

The AI coding tool I had to learn, Claude Code, normally talks to its vendor's servers, but it can be pointed at a
proxy on your own laptop instead. Mine was LiteLLM, open source, and several providers offered free tiers for
developers. So: the tool talks to the proxy, the proxy talks to whatever models I choose, and the keys are mine.

It took me four evenings and one night, the night mostly spent installing things into the wrong Python.

I found an example configuration on the internet, copied it, put my keys in, and changed the model names. There was a line in it that said where the proxy should listen, and a line, commented out,
that would have made it demand a password. I read the model names very carefully. I did not read those two lines at
all.

Then, because I was enjoying myself and had not enjoyed myself in a long while, I wrote a little applet for the Trinity
panel: a dot that is green when the proxy is running, red when it has failed, grey when it is stopped. It was C++, it
was small, and it was entirely unnecessary. I loved it as you love a thing you made when nobody asked you to make
anything.

Later I took the proxy apart, kept what I needed, and added what I missed: a log of every call with its tokens and its
cost, and a monthly cap after which the proxy simply says no. I called the result kiko. The name does not matter. What
mattered was that I could see, every morning, what the night had cost.

The vendor does not support any of this; it is a hack, and it broke twice after updates. I never sent my employer's code
through a free tier, which is paid for with your data: the workshop was for my own material, and I kept that rule the
way some people keep a fast.

## The first night

The first time the tool and the proxy spoke to each other, it was after one in the morning. The proxy was running in
one window. In the other, the tool had drawn its little frame and was waiting, the cursor blinking with the patience of
something that does not have to pay rent.

I asked it for something small and checkable: create a file called `hello.txt` containing today's date and the name of
the model you are.

Lines went past in the proxy window: a request in, a status code of 200. In the folder, a file appeared. It contained
the date, correctly, and the name of a model, incorrectly. The model had named itself after a different model, a more
expensive one, from another company, with complete serenity.

I laughed out loud in an empty flat. It was the first time I had heard myself laugh in the flat since April, and it
sounded strange, as if a neighbour had done it.

I turned round to tell somebody. The kitchen door was open. There was one cup on the drying rack, and I had washed it
myself.

I made tea in it and sat with the laptop on my knees and the dot on the panel glowing green. Green, in my experience,
is the colour things are just before they go red. That night I let it be green.

The column in the spreadsheet said four months and a little, if the probation failed. Eleven days of that were on my
knees, warm, with a scratch shaped like a river. I thought it a fair price.

I had three months.
