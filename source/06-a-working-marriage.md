# Chapter 6: A Working Marriage

In Douglas Adams's stories there is an electronic guidebook for hitchhikers across the galaxy, and early on Adams
explains why it sold better than the great, serious encyclopaedia of its universe. It was cheaper, and it told you on
its cover not to panic. Those are his reasons. The third is mine: it was often wrong, sometimes spectacularly, but it
was wrong in a pleasant voice, and nobody seemed to mind.

I read that again in my second year at the freight company, in one of the paperbacks I had refused to sell, with an
agent session open on the laptop beside me, and I recognised the product.

Adams wrote it as satire. I was using it to maintain a customs integration.

## The most tormented soul

There is a creature in Adams called Gargravarr, and I tell him from memory, which is not always Adams. He is a disembodied voice who guards the Total Perspective Vortex on Frogstar World B, and he is having a trial separation from his own body, which he expects to end in divorce. They wanted different things from life. His soul wanted only fishing: still water in the early morning, a line in the water, and perhaps not even a fish. His body wanted parties and sex and more of everything, and then more again. Each spoiled the other's pleasure with great efficiency.

Once they tried both at once, and it was a disaster. The body went its way, and the soul became a floating voice, watching. Gargravarr still loves fishing, exactly as he did before. He can only watch. Someone else holds the rod.

I thought about him a good deal in my second year, because the work had become a marriage of that kind. I wanted what the soul wanted: slow attention, to understand the systems I answered for and to know what was being done in my name. The machine has no wants, but it has needs the way an engine has needs: a great deal of text, fresh every time; clear tasks; tokens; and speed, a long stream of small actions, which is the opposite of slow attention. When I let it run fast, I lost the thread. When I checked every step myself, the work doubled rather than halved.

There are two ways out of that misery, and I watched colleagues take both. Some tried the tools, hated the noise, and went back to doing everything by hand, with great dignity. The work kept arriving, more of it each month, and the board began to ask why their tickets took so long. Others went the other way.

## The fourth cup

Steven was the other way. Steven, from the next desk, who had made the coffee at half past five in my first month while I put back the clients I had thrown away, now ran at the machine's speed: four sessions at once, approving whatever came back green. For a few months his name was on slides. Then on a Friday evening he merged a change he had not read. It emptied a table of shipment records.

I was in the building that Saturday, because the flat was quiet and the office was quieter in a better way. At noon he was at his desk, grey in the face, with three empty cups in a row by the keyboard, typing restore commands from a backup that somebody, by luck, had once tested. He read each one out before pressing Enter. I put a fourth cup down. He did not look up. He said thanks to the screen.

At four the count matched. He sat back and looked at the ceiling. "They'll let me go."

"Hanne doesn't let people go for one Friday."

"Not for the Friday." He picked up the fourth cup, found it cold, drank it anyway. "You know when I learned to drive? My uncle's Escort. Ninety-five. No, ninety-six, it was the year of the floods, the road by the river was shut and we had to go round by the brickworks. The instructor smelled of onions, and he had the second pedal on his side. The brake. And the first week I'm sweating, I'm doing everything, mirror, clutch, indicator, the whole song. The second week I notice, when it gets tight, his foot goes down before mine. The third week I'm not braking at all. Why would I brake? He brakes. I'm steering and listening to the radio, there was a song that summer, I can't remember it, it went da-da, da-da-da. And then the exam. Different man in the seat. My uncle's car. No pedal on his side. And we come to the first roundabout, the big one by the bus station, and I'm sitting there, and the foot doesn't —" He stopped and looked at the screen, where the count matched. "Anyway. I failed. Twice. The second one was a woman, she was worse." He put the cup down. "Since March I've been sitting there. Somebody upstairs is going to ask what the man in the driver's seat is for."

I could have said something encouraging. I had several things ready. Instead I told him about the letter, and the script.
I had never told anyone at the freight company. I did not know I was going to tell him until I had.

"I'm afraid of the same thing," I said. "Every Friday. I read the diffs because of it, not because I'm wise."

He looked at me then, for the first time that day. "Does it help?"

"It helps on Mondays."

He did not ask the next day. On the Thursday he came in early. "Sit here." It was a small change, and it took him an hour. He found two things the machine had not mentioned and wrote them on paper, slowly, the way you write when you have forgotten how your own hand works. When he was done he said thanks to the screen, caught himself, turned round and said it to me.

He is still here. It took him a month to trust the habit. He reads his diffs now, every line, with a wooden school ruler held against the screen, a ruler with somebody else's name scratched into the end of it, and he moves it down one line at a time.

## The terms

After that Saturday, Hanne asked me to write rules for the team. I wrote my own first, on the back of a printed
shipping manifest, and pinned them above my desk.

*It does:* read wide, draft, try, show what it did.

*I do:* decide what is worth doing, hold the facts, read what matters, sign.

*Neither alone:* production, keys, anything sent outside, the word "final".

*Mine:* the mornings, with no sessions open. No approvals after six.

I break them about once a month. Last month it was the six o'clock rule: a small change, approved at a quarter to
eight, that put a wrong time zone into one carrier's delivery estimates for a morning. That line on the manifest is
underlined now, in a different pen.

An ordinary day under them goes like this. At eight I open nothing. I read the ticket, the carrier's specification,
forty pages written by a committee that was paid by the comma, and the old handler, with a pencil. By half past nine I
have a page of notes, and one line on it is the real question: the new format allows a shipment several customs
references, and our database has a column for one. No machine would have asked that.

At ten I open a session, with the notes and a brief, and ask it to stop and propose before it touches the database. At
two there is a branch with nine small commits. I give it to a fresh session of a different model, told to assume
something is wrong. It finds that one of the sample messages in the test folder was written by the first session and
not taken from the carrier's specification, so the round-trip test proved only that the machine agreed with itself. I
put in the carrier's real sample. The test fails. We fix the reason. At six I close the laptop with something else
half-finished, and leave it half-finished.

I have had about two hundred of those days now.

## A year at a time

When the first year of the contract ran out, I asked Hanne for a permanent one. I asked on the proper form, and then,
because forms are where requests go to be kept on file, in person, in her office, with the door open.

She did not make me wait. She had the decency to say it to my face.

"One more fixed-term year," she said. "The board wants a part of the team to stay flexible. You are in that part."

I said that by the analyst's own figures the counting procedure alone had saved them more than my salary.

"I know. I showed them the figures." She capped her pen. "It isn't about the figures. If there is another round of
cuts, flexible people cost less to let go. I would rather you knew that than guessed it."

I asked whether she had argued.

"Yes," she said. "I argued, and I lost." She did not say it to be thanked, and I did not thank her.

"If the programme goes well," she said, "there may be another conversation." I did not ask which programme. I had
heard the word upstairs.

I signed. The copy came back to my desk that afternoon, in an envelope, without a paperclip.

## The tree

That winter the company announced that everything would move to the cloud: every service, every queue, the old
customs handlers and the database with the column for one reference. There was a video, and a date, and a programme
with a name made of two words that had never met before.

Steven read the announcement at the coffee machine. Put his phone back in his pocket.

"Do you know the one about the sparrows?" I did not. "Sparrows, right, on a tree. They shit on it, a heap grows under, stinks like hell. So they hold a meeting. Argue for hours. Decide: new tree. Everybody's busy, packing, running about. Only the little one sits on his branch, thinking. His mother says, what are you thinking about, it's fine, we move and it'll be lovely. And he says, very sad: when we move, do we take our arses with us, or leave them on the old tree?"

I laughed so loudly that someone from finance looked round. Steven took his cup back to his desk.

That weekend I moved the proxy at home into a user account of its own, as I had told myself I would since the library.
The new account started cleanly on the first try, which never happens and made me suspicious, so I searched the copied
folder for 0.0.0.0. There was one, in a backup of the configuration from the first month, with its friendly note about
the password you could enable if you liked. I deleted it, and searched again, and then searched the old account as
well.

## John

That spring I met John at the bus stop by the market, at a quarter past five. I was going in early; he was finishing.

The warehouse had put in a scanning tunnel the winter before, a frame that reads all six sides of a carton at once, with no breaks and no half sandwiches. His line was gone. Now he drove a bakery van, three to five, and smelled of bread. It is the least fair thing I know, and it suited him.

He had a letter in his jacket, folded small, from the employment office, about a retraining course. "Read it. You read things for a living."

It was four paragraphs long. Three of them were about how much the office valued his application. The fourth said the
course was full and that he had been placed on a waiting list.

"Good or bad?" said John.

"Bad. The verb is in the last paragraph."

He took the letter back and looked at the fourth paragraph, as if it might have changed while I held it. "You know the
one about the cannibal? One in the warehouse used to tell it. Mick, nights, the one with the tooth. Cannibal comes
home, sits down to dinner. Wife puts the plate in front of him. Same thing as yesterday. Same as the day before. Fourth
time this week. And he looks at the plate, and he says: 'Human. All too human.'"

He laughed at it himself, once, through his nose, and folded the letter back into its square.

I knew where the last three words had been before they were in the warehouse. I did not say.

I said that I could show him the laptop, the workshop, that it was not as hard to start as it looked, that a man who
could read a fourteen-digit code off a wet label at three in the morning could read an error message. He looked at me kindly, as if I had offered to teach him to swim on a dry day.

"Bread goes out at three," he said. "Three to five, the villages, then the van's washed by six or he docks me. I'm on
the list for the course. They said spring. They said spring last —"

He did not finish. I wrote my number on the back of the envelope. He put the letter back in his jacket, went to the van and came back with a loaf. "End of the round. Leftovers go in the bin." He gave it to me. My bus came. I
carried the loaf to the freight company under my arm, like a man with somewhere to be, and I did not know how to feel
about any of it, and I still do not.

## The paper

On a Thursday in that same spring there was a message from Helen, the first in a year. The last paper. A lawyer's
office, Friday, ten o'clock, if that suited me.

She was there before me, as she always had been. A new coat, the colour of wet sand; her hair shorter. In the waiting
room, before we were called, she asked whether I still had the Adams paperbacks.

"Nobody would buy them," I said.

"Is that why you kept them?"

I did not answer, and she let me not answer.

We sat side by side in front of a desk while the lawyer read the agreement aloud at the speed of a man reading the terms
of a loan, and then we signed in the same three places, she first, then me, with the same pen. It took a quarter of an
hour. I know because I looked at the clock when we sat down; it is a habit.

On the pavement she said there was a café across the road, if I had time. I had time.

She asked about the job. I told her about the notebook, and the owl with the moustache, and she laughed, properly, the
laugh I had not heard since well before April. She told me about her own: an office in a school in her sister's town,
a small flat with a balcony, a tomato plant. She showed me a photograph of the tomato plant. It was losing.

Then she turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, and I knew she was counting something.

"Are you all right?" she said.

I did not answer at once. I thought about it. She watched me think about it, and something moved in her face.

"Didn't you use to say yes before I'd finished?" she said.

"I've been trying to stop."

"And?"

"Mostly. The flat's quiet. The contract is a year at a time. I sleep." I thought again. "Mostly all right."

She nodded. Another quarter turn. "That morning," she said. "If you'd let me finish, and asked me which, what would I
have said? I've thought about it a lot. I don't know."

"Neither do I," I said, and that was true as well.

She paid, because she had asked. Outside she buttoned the coat and said, "Look after yourself, will you?"

I waited until I was sure she had finished. Then I said yes.

## The number

In July the programme put its plan on a slide, and the slide went round before the board saw it. Most of it was
arrows. In the middle, in a box of its own, it said: "AI-augmented integration team: 5". I had read those words
before, in front of my old job's title, in the advert a matching engine had once chosen for me. On the next slide there was a
table of names in two columns. Steven's was in the second.

Hanne asked me into her office, and left the door open.

"The board wants an owner for the integration layer," she said. "Permanent. Technical owner. Your name under the
plan." She did not say the word five. It was on the screen behind her.

I asked for a day.

I ran my own procedure on it, all five lines, the underlined one last. The number had no command under it. Nobody
had counted anything; somebody had divided. Above my desk, seven of the twelve index cards were still blank; nobody
replaces a client in a building that is moving. Seven carriers with private habits that nobody had written down, and a
team of five who would be expected to know them by spring.

I wrote my own estimate on one page, with three lines under it: how it was counted, what was not known, and the
command that made it. My figure was not five.

The next morning Hanne asked whether I had an answer for the board, which did not ask twice. I let her finish.

"No," I said, at the volume I use for status meetings, and put the page on her desk.

She read the three lines for a long time. Then she capped her pen.

"You know what it means for the flexible part," she said.

"Yes."

Steven was at the coffee machine when I came out. He asked if I wanted one, and I said yes before he had finished,
which I still do with coffee.

That evening I opened the old spreadsheet, the one from the year without work. The column called "months left" was
still there, hidden, with the last number in it. I did not change the number. I unhid the column.

In August my contract came back to my desk in an envelope, unchanged. Still the fixed-term year. The board likes to look again. On top of it,
with a paperclip, was my page with the three lines. The board decides about the five in October.

## Two beers

The paper from the lawyer is in my jacket, folded in three. I have not decided where to keep it.

There are two cups on the rack at home again. I bought the second in the winter, for nobody in particular.

On the first Friday of the month I leave at 16:00, which is still working time, and drink two pints at the place behind
the market; Friday is the craftsman's day, and the bosses look the other way.

It began with a phone call. John rang in September. I had written my number for him at the bus stop, on the back of the employment office's brown
envelope, and he had kept the envelope. "Friday. The place behind the market, with the green door. After 16:00. You read
things, you can read the menu." Steven came because I mentioned chips.

The place behind the market has six tables, a television with the sound off, and a landlady who starts pulling two pints
when she sees you in the door, before you have asked, on the theory that you will.

"What are we drinking to?" said Steven.

"Some drink from sorrow," I said, "others from joy."

"And us?"

"Us, from 16:00."

We had two each and a plate of chips the size of a hubcap.

I had not seen John since the spring. He had lost weight and gained a jacket, a proper one, navy, with a badge on the
pocket that I could not read.

He told it the way he scanned: one thing, then the next, no commentary. The course came through in June. Office
administration, six weeks, a laptop each, older than mine. He got the certificate; he kept it in the van, in a plastic
sleeve, in case anybody asked. Nobody asked. Four interviews in July: a builders' merchant, a car-parts warehouse, a
firm that sold office chairs, the council. At the third one a young woman asked him about his energy levels.

"Energy levels," said John. "I said I get up at half two. She wrote something."

I drank some beer.

In August he took nights as security on a building site by the river: eight floors of concrete, a hut, a kettle, a
dog that belonged to the site. Boys came for the copper cable. He chased them twice, in the dark, over the rubble, at
fifty-eight, and caught nothing but a nail through the boot.

"Two in the morning," he said. "I go round the back, in the reeds, to do what you do. I'm standing there. And one
lands. A mosquito. Right on. There." He did not point; he did not need to. "And I've got my hand up, like this." He
lifted his hand off the table, the size of a shovel, the hand that had torn sandwiches in half for strangers, and held
it in the air over the chips. "And I stand. Ten seconds. Twenty. Me with this. Him with his little — " He looked at
the hand for a while and put it down flat on the table, very gently, next to his glass.

"When a mosquito lands on your balls, you understand that not every problem is solved by force."

Steven laughed so hard that he had to put his beer down and then could not find where he had put it.

"That's the cop," said Steven, when he could. "A copper, thirty years on the beat, fixes every problem with his fists. Then one day he finds his grown-up daughter's bag under her bed, and in it whips and handcuffs and the like."

He sat back. He let it sit. He had been waiting all evening for somebody to give him a reason.

"And the copper sighs: 'Well. A beating won't sort this one out.'"

John nodded, slowly.

Nobody said anything for a while. The landlady brought a second plate of chips.

"And now?" I said to John. "The jacket."

"Minibus." He touched the badge. It said the name of a village at the end of the old 94, which had stopped going there
in the cuts: six hundred people, a school, a doctor on Tuesdays and Fridays, and a village council whose chair's wife bought
her bread from his van. The council had found the money for a second-hand community minibus and needed somebody who knew the road.

"Six-forty from the church," said John. "Seven-ten the school. Eleven the doctor, Tuesdays and Fridays. Market Saturday,
eight. Back by one." He drank. "I made it. The timetable. In pencil, on the door of the shop. Pencil, so I can change
it."

"And the bread?"

"Three to five. Then the minibus. Then I sleep." He shrugged. "Four hours. It's enough. It was enough on the 94."

Steven told John about the cloud and showed him, with two fingers on the table, how he holds the ruler against the
screen, one line at a time. I told them where I was: a contract a year at a time, and the counting procedure from my
notebook, grown up, running every Saturday at dawn with a second model to doubt it.
Students still sent fixes to kiko, and some asked me for a reference. I said yes, after the question. That month it
was a boy who had fixed the macOS part, and he had an interview at Emil's company. I told him he could not be in better hands.

"So what are you now," said John, "a programmer?"

"Mostly."

He nodded at that too. The landlady took the empty plate.

At nine he stood up, because the bread goes out at three. He paid for the second plate of chips over our
objections, which he did not hear. Then he took a beer mat from the stack by the salt, turned it over, borrowed
Steven's pen and wrote on the back of it in square capitals, pressing hard enough to mark the table: SAT 8.00 CHURCH.
MARKET. BACK 13.00.

"In case," he said, and put it in front of me, and went out into the street with his hands in his navy pockets.

I put the beer mat in the inside pocket of my jacket, beside the paper from the lawyer.
