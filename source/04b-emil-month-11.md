# Emil, month 11

The team lead left in the spring, for a company with a better coffee machine and a worse product, and on his last day
he told Ruth that Emil should take the team. "He's the one everyone already asks," he said. "He communicates well."

Nobody asked whether Emil was the one who knew most. He was not; there were two people on the team who could read the
warehouse code in their sleep and occasionally did. But they did not write clear issues. One of them wrote commit
messages consisting entirely of the word "stuff". You cannot give a team to a man who writes "stuff".

Ruth told him in the small room with the drowning plant's sister in it. It was not a question, and he answered it
anyway: "Sure. Happy to."

So Emil became tech lead, with a raise and a title that fitted on one line of an e-mail signature,
which he changed the same afternoon and then looked at for longer than he would have admitted.

The spreadsheet from the bad year was still on the Mac. He opened it that evening and renamed the column. It had said
"Weeks until loan repayments start". Now it said "Weeks until loan repaid". The number in it was small and falling the
right way. He sat and looked at it as at a photograph of himself on a good day.

Then the calendar arrived.

It did not arrive all at once. A daily standup at a quarter past nine, fifteen minutes, which took twenty-five.
Refinement on Tuesdays. A sync with the platform team. A sync with product. A one-to-one with each of five people, every
fortnight, because a book Ruth had read said so. A planning meeting every second Monday, and on the Friday before it a
meeting to prepare the planning meeting. And on Wednesdays, at four, a half-hour he had made himself, titled "Prep:
Thursday sync", which was a sync about the sync.

Each one was reasonable. He could have defended any of them in front of anyone. He did, twice.

The Mac changed first. Not its hardware; its habits. To make room in the dock for the video-call application, the chat
application, the notes application and the application that connected the other three, he tidied. The terminal icon,
which had sat third from the left since the day he bought the machine, went into a folder he called "Other", along with
the editor and a game he had stopped playing. The folder had a grey icon that looked like a filing cabinet. It was a
filing cabinet.

The fan, which had once roared at two in the morning while he compiled things for no reason, now ran mainly during
calls, when the camera and three browser tabs and the chat all wanted it at once. It was a video-call machine with an
octopus on it.

He still coded at night, quietly now, because somebody was asleep in the next room. On Fridays, mostly. Some
Fridays.

In October a message came from the man with the freight company, the first in months. Somebody had opened an issue on
kiko: the macOS section had gone stale after a system update. Would Emil, who had written it, have time to look?

Emil read it between the platform sync and the product sync and felt something that he identified, after a moment, as
happiness. He typed: "Yes! Will look at it this weekend." He meant it. He put it in the calendar, Saturday at ten,
"kiko macOS", in green, because green was the colour he used for things that were his.

On Saturday at ten he was asleep. He had earned it. It had been a long week; there had been a sync on Friday at six
about a release, and the release had needed another sync. He woke at noon, saw the green block in the calendar, and
moved it to Sunday.

On Sunday it moved to the next weekend. On the next weekend it was no longer there. He could not remember deleting it.
