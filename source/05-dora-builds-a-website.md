# Chapter 5: Dora Builds a Website

On the ground floor of my building there is a shop the width of a wardrobe, where a woman called Dora mends shoes,
cuts keys, replaces watch batteries and, if you stand there long enough, tells you what is wrong with the government.
She has been there longer than I have lived upstairs. The sign over the door was painted by hand and has faded to the
colour of weak tea. The key-cutting machine screams like a dentist's drill, and she runs it without ear protection, on
principle, the principle being that the machine should be ashamed, not her.

One evening in my eighth month at the freight company, coming home, I found her in the doorway holding her phone at
arm's length, as if it had insulted her.

"A customer asked for my website. I said I haven't got one. She looked at me like I'd said I haven't got a nose."

I said, without thinking, that I could make her one.

She gave me the look she gives customers who want a heel replaced by tomorrow morning. "So when it breaks, I call you? When you move, I call who?"

It was a better question than most I have heard in architecture meetings. I thought about it on the stairs, and came
back down.

"No," I said. "You make it. On my old laptop. I sit next to you and tell you what to type. When you are finished, you
know how it works, and when it breaks, you know where to look."

She laughed, which is not the same as saying no.

The laptop was the one from the tyre shop, with the workshop already on it; that part needs a helper the first time.
Everything after it, Dora did herself, four evenings at the counter after closing, the laptop balanced between a box of
heels and a jar of keys. The steps live with the tool's own documentation. What follows is what happened.

## Evening one: the paper

We did not touch the laptop on the first evening.

I gave Dora a sheet of paper and asked her to write everything a customer should know. Not how it should look; what it
should say. The name of the shop. What she does. What she does not do. When she is open. Where she is. How to reach
her. What a few common jobs cost.

She wrote for twenty minutes, crossed out, wrote again, and complained throughout that nobody had made her write an
essay since school and that school had not been worth it either. At one point she asked: "Should I put since 1994?"

"Is it true?"

"It's nearer 1996. But 1994 sounds better."

"Then the machine will love it," I said. "It loves things that sound better."

She looked at me for a long moment, crossed it out and wrote 1996. Then she added, in smaller letters underneath,
"probably 1997", and crossed that out as well, because there are limits.

She had made tea, in two mugs from the shelf behind the heels, and lit a cigarette. I had the customers' stool, the
one with a split in the vinyl. The door stood half open for the smoke. The ashtray was the lid of a polish tin.

"And before 1996?" I said.

She did not look up. She was writing the price of a heel.

"The works. Twenty years."

She did not say which works, and I did not ask.

"Watches," she said. "I set the movements. Under a lens, all day, with tweezers. Twelve hundred a month."

"And then?"

"Then a letter." She wrote the price of a zip. "And a box for my things. And a clock."

"A clock."

"For the wall. With the director's signature on the face. So I'd remember who."

She pointed with the cigarette at the back wall, without turning round. I had waited in that shop a hundred times and
never looked at it. A round clock, a cream face, a signature in gold, too small to read from the stool. Under it, in
plain capitals I could read: FOR LONG AND LOYAL SERVICE. Dust lay along the top of the frame, and a cobweb ran from it
to the wall. The hands said twenty past four. They had said it for a long time.

"I didn't want it at home," she said. "Five pounds, a clock like that, in the Chinese shop. The engraving cost twenty.
I'd have engraved it better myself. Throw it out? Five pounds is five pounds."

She tapped ash into the polish tin.

"The money they gave me paid for this shop," she said. "The lens I took from the works. Nobody asked for it back."

She still has it. It lives in the drawer under the counter, with her phone, and she uses it on the teeth of
keys. She wipes it before she puts it back. There is not a mark on the glass.

"You sell batteries," I said.

"Two shelves."

"It doesn't want one?"

"It wants one." She put the cigarette out in the lid. "Twelve hundred a month I set for them, and every one of them
ran. Let it stand. Let it see how it feels."

She went back to her paper. My tea had gone cold some time before. I kept the mug.
It was the first evening since Helen left that I had spent in a room with somebody else in it, doing a thing
together, with the light on. I did not say so. It is not the kind of thing you say in a shoe repair shop.

Above her head the clock said twenty past four, as it does now.

I typed her page into a plain text file, spelling included, and read it back to her. She corrected two prices, and the
price of a watch battery twice, because she had raised it in her head while I was reading. The file was called
`content.txt`. Everything on the site would come from it. Anything on the site that was not in it would be a mistake,
however well it was written.

## Evening two: the first page

She typed every command herself, slowly, while I spelled them out.

The terminal offended her at first sight. A black window with a blinking line, waiting. Her first command was wrong:
she typed a space where there should not be one, and the machine answered with a sentence of complete indifference.
She read the sentence aloud, then read it again, slower, as she reads the small print on a battery packet, and found
her own mistake in it before I could point. I said nothing. I have trained people who took a month to start reading
error messages. She took four minutes, and she was cross with the machine the whole time, which I have come to think is
the right attitude.

Halfway through, a man came in with a boot whose sole was hanging off like a tongue. She closed the laptop lid on the
half-typed command, without saving anything, without asking me, served him, took his boot and his money, wrote him a
ticket, and opened the lid again. The command was still there, waiting. She looked at it as if it had been rude to
wait.

"It doesn't get bored, does it. The man with the boot was bored, I'm bored, the whole country's bored, you go to the council and the girl behind the glass is bored to death, and this thing just sits there with its little line going." She finished the command. Enter.

I sat on the customers' stool, which was my place now, and spelled out the next command.

A folder. Git, to give the folder a memory. A brief for the agent, which she dictated and I tidied: only the facts in
the file, plain pages, phone first, one step at a time and then stop. Then the first snapshot, before any website existed. She asked why we were saving nothing.

"So that we always know what we asked for," I said, "separately from what we got."

She started the agent. On the first evening, before she had even seen it, she had called it "your robot"; by the second
it had become "the clerk", and stayed the clerk. Her first request was small: one page with everything in the file, no
colours yet, then stop.

The clerk asked permission to create a file. Dora read the question aloud, all of it, in the voice she uses for
reading out other people's guarantees, and said yes.

A few seconds later there was a page in the browser, served from the laptop to itself and to nobody else. It was plain: black text, white background, blue links, the look of the web before anyone had
opinions about it. She found it beautiful in an honest way, the way a well-resoled shoe is beautiful.

Then she read it against her sheet of paper, line by line, with her finger.

Halfway down, the page said the shop offered "orthopaedic adjustments".

She does not. It was not in the file. The clerk had added the likely next thing. One line among forty, in a sensible place, in a sensible tone.

"Ask it why," I said.

She asked it why. It apologised and said it had "inferred a common service".

"Inferred," said Dora. "Take it out." Then to me: "It does this often?"

"Often enough that you read with your finger."

Before saving, I showed her the command that lists what has changed since the last snapshot, in red and green. The
clerk did not write that list; git did. She checked that the orthopaedic line was gone and that nothing else had
moved, as carefully as she counts a customer's change. Then she saved the snapshot, with a message saying what and why.

## Evening three: making it look like hers

She asked for colours: the brown of old leather, a light warm background, large text. The first attempt was too small on
the phone, and the headings were a brown that looked, she said, "like something on the bottom of a shoe I'd refuse to
fix". Two more rounds, each one small, each one looked at, each one compared and saved.

Then she asked the clerk to make the opening hours "stand out". It produced a flashing yellow box.

We watched it flash for a while in silence, as you watch a car alarm in the street at night, waiting for someone
whose responsibility it is. Then I showed her the command that throws away everything since the last snapshot. She
typed it. The box was gone. She sat back and looked at the screen.

"Where was that forty years ago," she said. "Forty years I could have used that. My whole marriage."

She used it three more times that evening, once on purpose and twice to see it work again.

Then the photograph of the shop front. A phone photograph carries inside it, invisibly, the exact place where it was
taken; we stripped that out of a copy. She asked what else a photograph knows about her. I told her. She went quiet for a moment and then
said: "I'll be having words with my phone."

The clerk added the picture and proposed a description for people who cannot see images: "A charming traditional
cobbler's shop with a welcoming atmosphere." Dora changed it to "Shop front: a narrow door with a hand-painted sign
reading Dora, Shoes and Keys."

"Charming," she said. "Who says charming about himself? The customer says charming, if he wants, after, when he's paid. You say charming about yourself, that's how they put you in the paper."

Then she did the thing everybody does once. She opened the content file herself and added a line: "The best prices in
the district." She asked the clerk to put it at the top, in large letters. It did, at once, without a word of doubt.

"Is it true?" I said.

"Probably."

"The key kiosk at the station?"

She went quiet. The next morning, on my way to the bus, I went to the station and asked the price of a copy of my
flat key. I told her that evening. She said a word I will not print, about the kiosk and its owner's family, opened
the content file, and took the line out. Then she asked the clerk to take it off the page, and checked that it had,
with her finger.

"The machine would have let me." It would have let her say she was the Queen of Sheba. "It's like the council," she said. "They let you write anything on the form. Then they fine you." She saved it, with the message 'removed lie', and it is still there in the history, between 'hours' and 'brown'.

## Evening four: a stranger's eyes, and the world

Dora closed the session and started a new one, and for this one I pointed the workshop at a different model from a
different company. It got no history, only a reviewer's brief: you did not build this; change nothing; check every
fact and number against the content file, character by character; report a numbered list.

Dora watched it work with her arms folded. "A second clerk. To check the first clerk." I said it
was more like asking a customer from another street to read your price list before you hang it in the window. She said
nobody from another street would ever read her price list, because nobody from another street could be trusted with
her prices. But she did not stop it.

It found three things. A skipped heading level. Grey footer text too pale to read. And the phone number.

The number on the page was right. But on a web page a phone number is two things: the text you see, and the hidden
link a phone dials when you tap it. The clerk had typed them separately. In the hidden one, two digits
were swapped.

Dora took off her glasses, put them on again, and read the line on the screen a third time. Then she took out her own
phone and tapped the number on the draft page. It rang. A man answered, somewhere across the city, and said hello
twice. She hung up on him.

"So," she said. "My website looks at you with my number and calls somebody else."

She fixed it herself, as she now fixed everything: one request, look, compare, save. Then she fixed the heading
and the footer. Then she rang the number again from the page, and her own phone, in the drawer under the counter,
began to ring, and she let it ring for a while out of satisfaction.

Then she published it.

Before she would make the account, she wanted to know who owned the place where her site would live. A large company,
I said, that lends space to small projects free. And when they stop lending it? Then the whole site, with every
snapshot, was still in the folder on her laptop, and a dozen other places would take it tomorrow. She thought about that for a while, filing a burr off
a key blank that did not have one.

"So it's like the flat," she said. "Landlord puts the rent up, sells the building, the government does what it likes, but the wardrobe's mine, and when I go the wardrobe goes on the van. Yes? Like that?"

"Like that," I said. My own contract ran a year at a time. The notebook was in my bag.

She made the account, with the e-mail address the site offers to hide the real one; she took it with the satisfaction
of somebody handing out a key that opens the front door and not the drawer.

Then one command to send the snapshots up, a minute of waiting. She went out of the shop, onto the pavement,
turned off her wireless so that she would see what strangers see, and opened the address on her phone. The shop front appeared, the narrow door, the hand-painted
sign, under a sky that was the same colour as the one over our heads.

We did not add a contact form. We did not add anything to count her visitors.

"What do I want to count them for," Dora said. "The taxman counts me. The council counts me. Let them come in with a shoe, I'll count them then." She put the jar of keys back where the laptop had been, which was where it lived.

## Customers

Four evenings. About three hours of actual work, the rest being tea, interruptions and heels. One page, a
stylesheet and a photograph, with nothing on it that is not true.

On the fifth day a woman came into the shop and said she had found it on the internet. Dora told me this on the stairs
with the restraint she keeps for a watch that has started again: pleased, and listening for it to stop.

She borrowed the laptop for a month. Then she went to the man behind the tyre shop, without telling me, and came back
with one of her own: grey, heavier than mine, with a sticker on the lid from a company that no longer exists. She made
me sit beside her while she set up the workshop on it, from the sheet of steps I had written out for her by hand, and
she typed every line of that too, and when it worked she gave me my laptop back with a packet of chocolate biscuits, which in her
economy is a formal receipt.

In the winter a young man came in out of the sleet with a tablet held against his chest like a hymn book. I was on
the customers' stool, waiting for a watch battery.

He was not a customer. He was from a company that lists businesses, and he was pleased for her. Her website had been
noticed, he said; the shop had been picked up. He turned the tablet round on the counter so that she could see.

It was a page about the shop. It had her name, her street, and a photograph of the door taken from across the road, in
summer, by somebody she had never met. Under it was a paragraph she had not written. She read it with her finger, and
stopped once, at a word, and went on.

Under the paragraph there were stars. Three, and most of a fourth. Four reviews.

"Who are they?"

The young man said they were customers.

"Name me one."

He could not; they had initials. One had written "ok". One had written that the shop was hard to find. The shop is
directly under my flat.

"Three point eight," said Dora. "Four people I never saw, giving me marks. My husband at least had his trousers off
first."

The young man went pink and moved on to the part he had come for. The page could be verified. For a small monthly
amount she could correct the description, answer the reviews, and have a green tick beside her name, so that people
would know the page was really hers.

"It is really mine," she said. "It's got my door on it."

He said he understood. He said it was generated automatically, from public sources, and that he could not change it
himself. Only the owner could, through the account.

"So you made the shoe without my foot in it," said Dora, "and now I pay you every month to let me stand in it."

He asked whether she would like to think about it, and put his card on the counter.

She did not pick it up. She finished my battery first, pressed the back of the watch shut with her thumb, held it to
her ear and gave it to me. Then she took the card, folded it once, and pushed it under the short leg of the
key-cutting machine, in place of the folded bus ticket that had been there for as long as I have lived upstairs. She cut a key to see
whether the machine still rocked. It did not.

The page is still up. It has six reviews now. It still says orthopaedic.

The next spring the man from the key kiosk at the station came into her shop. I was there, waiting for a heel. He was a
large man in a small jacket, and he stood just inside the door, as if the shop might charge him for coming further. His
daughter had shown him Dora's website on her phone. He wanted one. He asked who had made it, and looked at me.

"I did," said Dora. He went on looking at me. "I did," she said again, in the voice she uses for customers who want a heel by tomorrow morning.

He asked what she would charge to make him one. She put down the boot she was holding. "Charge? I don't make websites, what am I, a company? You make it. You sit there, on my laptop, and I tell you what to type, and you type it, and when it breaks you don't come running to me at seven in the morning, you know where to look. And you bring your own tea."

He laughed, and did not leave. They did it on Tuesdays after closing, the two of them at her counter,
his thick fingers on her keyboard, her voice spelling out commands. I was not invited. I heard about it on the stairs.
His first content file said "The best prices in the city". She sent him to the window to read her price list, and he
came back and took the line out himself, and wrote "Keys cut while you wait", which was true.

His site went up in April. On two jobs, a car key and a lock change, his prices were lower than hers, and now anybody
with a phone could see it. Two of her regulars went to the station. She told me this on the stairs too, flatly.

"You could lower yours," I said.

"I could." She did not. "They'll come back when the key doesn't turn."

One of them came back in May, on a wet Thursday. He had a padlock from his
allotment shed in a carrier bag, and in the padlock a key that went in and would not turn.

He put the original on the counter beside it. He did not say where the copy had come from. Dora did not ask.

The machine screamed. She put the new key into the padlock, and it turned.

She charged him the price on her website, to the coin, and wrote him a ticket. He paid without a word.

At the door he turned round and said that it was raining.

Dora looked past him at the street, as if it needed checking.

"It is," she said.

When he had gone she dropped the station copy into a tin under the counter. There were other keys in it already, not
many.

"Every key fits at the counter," she said, and put the lid back on.

The other one did not come back, and she did not mention him again.

A month after that she changed her Saturday hours. She did not call me. She opened her laptop, started a session,
asked the clerk for the change, read it with her finger, compared, saved and published. The new hours were on the website by Sunday night; on the door, in her own hand, on the back of a shoebox lid, they had been up since Friday.
