# Emil, year 3

By the third year Emil was an engineering manager, which meant that he had four tech leads, each with a calendar like
his old one, and a calendar of his own that he no longer read so much as obeyed. It came in colours. Green had once
been for things that were his. There was not much green now, and what there was had been put there by his assistant,
for lunch.

He was good at it. People liked working for him. He wrote the clearest planning documents on four floors. He protected
his teams from the reorganisations, mostly, by learning to speak the reorganisations' language better than the people
who designed them. When the September reorganisation undid the March one again, two of his tech leads said he was the
only reason they had not left. He believed them. He went home and could not sleep.

He interviewed people now, on Thursdays. Most interviews were forty-five minutes and ended on time.

The applicant on that Thursday was a student, final year, in a jacket that had been bought for the occasion and did not
yet know it. Nervous, fast, a young man who apologised at the end of his answers, where it cost nothing. He had brought
a laptop he did not need, as if it were a passport. On his CV, under "Open source", there was one line: a fix to the
macOS section of a small local proxy called kiko, with the test output pasted into the pull request. Under "References"
there was one name. Emil knew it.

The interview went well. Not well enough. The company was hiring two people that quarter and there were forty
applications, and this one was a year too early, and Emil knew within ten minutes that the answer would be no, and spent
the other thirty-five being kind about it without saying it.

At the end the student asked a question nobody had asked Emil in three years of interviews.

"If it's a no," he said, "could somebody tell me what to improve? Even ten minutes. I'd actually do it."

"I'm sorry," said Emil. "We don't have capacity for that."

He had a planning meeting at three.

The student thanked him and left with the laptop under his arm.

Emil did not call the name under "References".

That night, at a quarter to one, when his partner had gone to bed, he opened the Mac. The octopus had faded to a ghost.
He opened the folder called "Other", took out the terminal, and opened a private repository that nobody at work knew
about. It held the timetable tool from his student days, rewritten twice since, for no reason that could be defended in
any meeting. It had one commit a month. Never more. He had tried more once, and the next morning had been so tired at
the standup that his tech leads had asked if he was ill.

He wrote for an hour. Something small: the tool now understood timetables that changed in the middle of a term. It was
interesting. He looked up and did not know where the hour had gone.

He heard the bedroom door. He closed the lid, quickly, the way a man stubs out a cigarette he has promised to give up,
and sat in the dark with his hand on the warm aluminium.

"You coming?" said his partner from the hallway.

"Yes," said Emil. "One minute."

When the footsteps went away, he opened the lid again, typed "small fix", and pushed. Then he wrote the student's name
on a sticky note, stuck it to the edge of the screen, and went to bed.

In the morning the note was still there. He opened the calendar and put the student in it, Saturday at ten, in
green. He had a standup at a quarter past nine.
