# Emil, month 4

The Mac had belonged to a graduate who was leaving the country and wanted cash more than a fair price. It had a dent
in one corner, a key that stuck in cold weather, and a sticker of a cartoon octopus that Emil had tried to peel off and
only made sadder. He loved it as you love the first thing you paid for yourself, which is to say without looking
at it.

For eighty-three applications it had been a machine for filling in forms. At night it had been something else. At night
he wrote code because it was interesting, which is a reason nobody puts on a CV, because nobody would believe it. The
timetable tool, the half-finished game, a stranger's proxy that did not start on a Mac. He would look up at two in the
morning and not know where the evening had gone, and that was the whole of the pleasure: the evening going somewhere.

The company that took him made software for warehouses. Forty people on two floors, a kitchen with a coffee machine
that had opinions, a plant in reception that everybody watered and so it was slowly drowning. They gave him an
internship, a desk by the radiator and a ticket nobody else wanted: a carrier adapter that dropped one message in
every few thousand.

He found it in nine days. He wrote it up as the man with the freight company had once written his issue for help:
what happens, what should happen, how to make it happen on purpose, what done looks like. He pasted the output. Ruth,
who ran the integrations team, forwarded his write-up to everyone with one line on top: "This is how a ticket looks."

After that people sent him things to write up. Not to fix; to write up. A bug that two seniors had been arguing about
for a month, in the comments, at length, in the tone of an old marriage. Emil read both sides, wrote a page saying what
each of them meant, and the argument stopped by lunchtime. He was pleased.

By the review he had written up eleven bugs. Other people had fixed ten of them.

His three-month review had two lines that mattered. "Communicates very well." And, further down: "Technical depth:
developing."

He read the first line many times. He read the second once, and decided it was what they wrote for everybody.

He sent the first line to the man with the freight company, because he had been a reference and it seemed polite to
report on the investment. The answer came the same evening, two words: "Good. Congratulations." Emil read it as warm. It
was warm. It was also exactly two words.

In the fourth month Ruth called him into the small room with the drowning plant's sister in it.

"We'd like to offer you a permanent place," she said. "Junior, but permanent. And there's something else. The weekly
integrations sync needs somebody to run it. Agenda, notes, follow-ups. You're the obvious choice. You write so clearly."

The pay was better than anything in his spreadsheet had dared to assume. He did the arithmetic in his head while she
was still talking: the loan repayments, covered; the rent, covered; a new key for the cold-weather problem, perhaps.

"Sure," he said. "Happy to."

That night he went home, opened the Mac and wrote code until two, because it was interesting. The meeting was on
Thursday. It was Monday.
