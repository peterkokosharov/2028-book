# Chapter 4: What the Machine Is Made Of

In my fifth month at the freight company, while strangers' pull requests were still arriving at home, Hanne asked me
a question I could not answer.

"The board wants to know how it works," she said. "Not the sales version. They have had the sales version. They want to
know why it is sometimes brilliant and sometimes makes things up, and whether we should be afraid of it. And what it
will cost us. Ten minutes, next month. Can you do that?"

I said yes. Then I went back to my desk and tried to write the first sentence, and could not. I could use the machine.
I could catch it out. I had a notebook full of lessons. But every lesson in it described what the thing did, not why. I
knew which noises meant trouble, and nothing about the gearbox.

## The draft

I wrote three pages anyway and took them to Hanne. She read them standing at her desk, with a pen, and asked her
questions in the order the board would ask them.

"Why did it tell you its name was something else?"

On my first night with a free model, it had been handed instructions written for another company's model: you are
Claude. Then I had asked it who it was.

"It guesses what comes next," I said. "'You are Claude. Who are you?' What comes next is 'I am Claude.' It didn't
lie. It continued."

"So it does not know when it is wrong."

"It doesn't know anything, in the sense you mean. Usually what sounds right and what is right go together. When they
part, you get a smooth sentence with nothing under it."

"Forty-seven," said Hanne. She wrote something in the margin. "Why does it forget yesterday?"

"Because yesterday was never sent. Every time you write to it, the whole conversation goes again from the top. It reads
it, answers, and forgets."

"My people say it gets tired in the afternoon."

"By the afternoon the morning's instruction is a whisper in a stadium. I keep sessions short, and I write down whatever
has to survive. The text is the only steering wheel there is."

She underlined something. "The board is afraid of the word 'agent'."

"It asks before it touches anything," I said. "The danger is the man who stops reading the question." On Day 15 a
folder of configuration files had been saved because it asked me first, and because I, for once, read the question.

Hanne turned to the last page and back again. "And who can see the keys?"

There was nothing about keys in the draft.

## The library

The sample configuration I had copied at the very start told my proxy to answer anybody on the network, and the
password line was switched off, with a friendly note saying you could switch it on if you liked. At home, anybody meant
my phone and a television that mostly talked to itself.

In my second month, on a Saturday, I took the laptop to the public library, because the flat was cold and the library
was not. I sat by the window for four hours on the library's open network and worked.

On the Sunday afternoon, in the middle of a session, the provider refused me: daily quota exhausted. I had used perhaps
a tenth of it. I was furious, and I knew whom with. I went to a forum about free tiers and wrote a post. I called the
free tier a bait. I said it was a fraud dressed as generosity, that people like them got rich on people like us. I used
the word "us" with some feeling. Three people agreed with me within the hour. I remember being pleased, and I remember
refreshing the page to see whether a fourth had come.

On Monday evening I read the proxy's own log, which I should have read on Sunday before I wrote a word. There were a
few hundred calls on the Saturday afternoon that I had not made, from another machine in the library. Something
roaming the network looking for open doors had found mine, and had spent my quota with my keys.

I deleted the forum post, and did not tell the three who had agreed with it that they had been wrong too. I changed
every key I had, set the proxy to answer only my own laptop, and only with a password, and wrote "fix sample" on a list.
It stayed there for nine weeks.

Later I moved the keys into a file that only the proxy read, so that the agent never saw them, and said so, rather
proudly, in my notes. The reviewer, the cold one, asked one question. Which user does the agent run as?

As me. Everything ran as me. I asked the agent what was in that file. It asked permission to read it, politely, as it
asks permission for anything. If I had said yes with my hand and not my head, the keys would have gone out with the
rest of the conversation.

I had kept the keys out of the agent's pocket. They were still in the house, and the agent had the house keys.

So the line in the notes changed. The real wall, a separate account for the proxy, came later.

## The board

The ten minutes went well. I explained the guessing, the forgetting, the asking permission, and why a long session keeps
paying for its own beginning: in kiko's log I had found one that sent the same file forty times. Then the finance
director asked what it would cost if all twelve developers used it daily. I had prepared for that. I had taken a week of
my own usage from kiko's log, multiplied it out, and rounded down, because rounding down sounds modest. I said it was
roughly the price of a team lunch per person per month. The finance director wrote the figure down. Hanne, beside me,
nodded, which meant she had vouched for it.

I had not run the number past anyone. Not a second model, not a colleague, not my own procedure with its underlined
line: never send a number without the command that made it. It was only an estimate, and anyway it was mine.

The first month's invoice was nearly four times the figure.

The second board meeting was on a Tuesday at ten, in the long room on the fourth floor where the radiators knock. The
finance director put my slide from the first meeting on the screen, and beside it,
at the same size, the invoice. Two numbers. No colour, no arrow. He had not needed an arrow.

He did not raise his voice. He explained the difference correctly, in front of everybody. My week had been short
sessions on cheap models, a habit from a free tier where every word counted. My colleagues worked as anyone works when
nobody has taught them otherwise: long sessions on the large model, whole folders read in, one session left open for
three days and fed every morning. He had read my own slide on cost more carefully than I had.

I looked down the table and thought of the twelve, not as a figure but as desks. The one who ate at his keyboard. The
one with a child's drawing of a dog taped to his monitor. The one who said good morning to everybody, every morning,
including the plant. I had sat among them all those months, and I had not shown one of them how to close a session.

"It isn't the tool," I said.

I had not been asked anything. The finance director stopped, with the remote control still raised, and waited.

"The number was right for the way I work. I measured me. Nobody showed them how. I didn't show them. That was mine to
do, and I didn't do it, and if they worked the way I work, it would be the number on my slide. It would be the lunch.
It's not the tool."

I said it at the volume I use for status meetings, in the order I would have put it on a slide, and stopped when it was
finished, because it was finished. Somebody's phone buzzed face down on the table and was left there.

"Thank you," said the finance director. "That is useful. We will hold you to it." He wrote something down. Then he
moved on to the next item, which was the car park.

The agenda lay under my right hand. Along its margin there was a row of small crescents, pressed in with a thumbnail
while the finance director had been explaining my slide to the room, evenly spaced, as if somebody had been keeping
count. There were eleven. I counted them during the car park.

Hanne did not look at me. Her pen lay on the table in front of her, capped, on top of her closed notebook, and it stayed
there through the car park, the canteen contract and any other business. When the meeting ended she picked it up, put
it in her inside pocket, and held the door for the man behind her.

Since then the finance director sends any number of mine to one of his analysts before he reads it.

The evening of the meeting I ran my own procedure on my own estimate, all five lines. A fresh model, told there was an
error, found it in a quarter of an hour. I wrote a one-page note for my colleagues, sat with each of the twelve for
twenty minutes over the next fortnight, and the next month's invoice came down by half.

That month the cloud was first mentioned upstairs. Five of the twelve index cards above my desk were full by then.

## What does not change?

The real end of those weeks came a week later, at eight minutes to two in the morning, when my phone walked itself
across the bedside table on vibrate.

"Paul. Infra, on call," said the phone. "The big carrier's queue isn't draining. Service is up. Everything's green.
It's your layer. Sorry."

I knew Paul from the coffee machine, where he read change requests and wrote their numbers on the back of his hand.
By four minutes past two I was at the kitchen table in a jumper over my pyjamas, and he had the voice of a man woken at
the start of a dream he had been looking forward to. Messages from the carrier were piling up, forty a minute, and the
service said it was fine and did nothing.

The carrier's containers go to the port on the morning train, and their customs declarations had to be with the border
authority by six. A container that missed the train stood at the terminal for a day, at the carrier's cost, under a
clause that somebody in legal had once been proud of. There were eleven hundred messages in the queue.
Somewhere at a gate on the edge of the city, I knew, men were standing by their cabs, smoking and swearing at a system
none of them had seen. This time it was mine.

At ten past two I asked the agent. It answered in under a minute, in the voice of a consultant who charges by the day.
The likely cause, it said, was too few workers for the load; raise them from twenty to sixty.

"Paul, is the load higher than yesterday?"

"Same as yesterday. Same as last Tuesday."

I passed it on. The agent thanked me for the clarification. In that case, it said, the service was most probably short
of memory.

"Memory's flat," said Paul, before I asked. "All night."

The remaining explanation, the agent said, was the carrier failing now and then; it wrote forty lines that would
simply try again, with a test. The test passed. It would have passed whatever the carrier was doing, because the
same session had written both.

Each answer was reasonable in general. Each arrived complete, numbered, with the code attached.

At twenty to three Hanne rang. The carrier's night manager had rung her; she had been asleep, and did not sound it.

"I have him on the other line," she said. "He wants an estimate. I understand that you may not have one. I would
like the one you would put your name to, and I will tell him that it is an estimate."

I opened my mouth to say "before five". It was a good number. I had nothing under it at all.

"I don't know yet," I said. "I'll know what it is by three. I'll ring you at three."

There was a short pause. "Three o'clock is an answer I can use. Thank you." She hung up.

Two and a half thousand in the queue.

At a quarter to three the agent came back with more confidence than before, not less. The safest thing, it said, was to
restart the service: the messages would move at once, and the cause could be found in daylight. It was numbered. It
had a heading. It was the most sensible thing anybody had said all night.

"Restart's two minutes," said Paul. "Say the word."

I could hear him type the command. Then the typing stopped, and I knew his finger was on the key, waiting for me.

And I felt it. I felt exactly what Steven would describe to me months later, on a Saturday, with three empty cups beside
his keyboard: that the machine knew what it was doing and I was in its way. All I had was a feeling that something was
wrong, and a queue that grew by forty a minute while I sat in my kitchen having it. Every minute I did not say yes was
a minute I had taken from the carrier and the drivers and Hanne, for a feeling.

"Ye—" I said.

The sound was out. I heard it go down the line.

A program that is alive and doing nothing is not broken. It is waiting. I had learned that fifteen years earlier, on a
program I had broken on purpose to see how it would die. A restart would make the waiting stop. The messages would
move, for a while, and whatever had stopped them would stop them again, at four, or at five, or at a quarter to six,
and the only evidence of what it was would be gone, because the evidence was the waiting itself.

"— wait," I said. "Don't. Don't restart it. Before anything, can you get me a thread dump?"

There was a pause on the line, a different one. "Two minutes."

He did not ask why.

A thread dump says what every worker inside a program is doing at that moment. It arrived: four thousand lines, and I
read it myself, the old way, scrolling with one finger while Paul breathed on the line and the kitchen clock, which is a
minute fast, moved past three.

Every one of the twenty workers was waiting for the same thing: a connection to the database. There were ten. Twenty
people, ten telephones. So I looked for who was on them.

Ten workers, each holding one, each waiting for an answer from the carrier. Not for a while. Just waiting. I read the
contractor's settings twice. They kept the lines to the carrier open between calls, to save time, and set no limit at
all on how long to wait for an answer.

I rang Hanne. "I know what it is. I don't know how long yet. I'll know by half past."

"Thank you," she said. "I will tell him that."

Then, to Paul: "Do you have anything between us and them that cuts quiet connections?"

The pause this time was long enough that I thought the line had gone. "Firewall," said Paul, slowly, waking up. "We
changed it in the spring. Quiet lines cut after five minutes. Change request's in the system. Nobody told your
contractor. Your contractor had gone."

So a question sent down a cut line simply vanished, and the worker that sent it waited, politely and for ever, for an
answer that was never coming. Ten of those, and twenty workers in line behind them, and the whole time the service told
everyone it was fine.

The dump also showed a search on the carrier table that read the whole table every time: not the cause of this night,
but of the next.

At a quarter past three I gave the agent the dump, the firewall's five minutes and the contractor's settings. Once it
had the facts it was excellent. It did in twenty minutes what would have taken me two hours, with a test that failed on
the old code and passed on the new, and it did not once mention the number of workers.

Paul said, "Emergency change form. I fill it, you sign." I signed it on the screen with my finger, in my pyjamas.

"Now," I said. "Now restart it."

"Restarting."

At eleven minutes to four the queue stopped growing. By four it was coming down. I rang Hanne and said: before six,
and this time I could tell her why. She asked for a written account by noon, with three lines underneath on how anybody
could check it. At twenty-six minutes past five the last declaration went to the border authority. I watched the
counter reach zero and stay there for a whole minute before I believed it.

Paul did not hang up at once. "Where did you learn to read those?" he said. "The dumps."

"In a job that doesn't exist any more."

He was quiet for a moment. "Could you show me? Not now. Some afternoon."

I said I would. I did, the following week, and the week after that, and by the summer he was reading them faster than
I was.

At six I opened the notebook at the kitchen table and wrote the date, and under it TODAY'S LESSON, and sat with the pen
over the line. Everything I had used that night was older than the machine, and older than the job I had lost. I left
the line empty, set the alarm for nine, and slept.
