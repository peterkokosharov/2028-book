# Thanks

Isaac Asimov, *The End of Eternity* (1955). For Andrew Harlan and the Technician's trade — the Minimum Necessary Change, the rule this book was cut by.

Douglas Adams, for the Guide, and most of all for Marvin.

Dan Simmons, most of all for Martin Silenus. The other pilgrims are good company too, and every one of them is a person.

Philip K. Dick — "Survey Team," *Flow My Tears, the Policeman Said* (1974), and *Time Out of Joint* (1959), my favourite, which I have always read as time when you are out of a joint.

Kurt Vonnegut, *God Bless You, Mr. Rosewater* (1965). Not the famous ones; this one, the most human. For Eliot Rosewater, who loved the people nobody needed.

Daniel Keyes, *Flowers for Algernon* (1959 story, 1966 novel). And Charlie Gordon, who knew more than all of us about being a person.

Roger Waters. I read *The Dark Side of the Moon*, *Wish You Were Here* and *The Final Cut* as poetry — "Time" above all, which I first read at fifteen, in Petya Dubarova's Bulgarian translation.

John Lennon, "In My Life." *There are places I remember.* The poem is genuine.

Kris Kristofferson and Janis Joplin, "Me and Bobby McGee." They showed me what freedom is. Janis's end showed me the price too.

Ken Kesey and Jack Nicholson, *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*. A man stays himself, and the price gets paid. Jack was unbelievable. And thanks to the Douglases, who got the film made.

The Rolling Stones, "Angie." Keith Richards for the music, Mick Jagger for the words. I think they found the formula, and it sounds good. When youth is gone, you start to value the love you lost. The kitchen scene in this book is exactly that.

Nina Simone, Bob Dylan and Eric Burdon of The Animals, "The House of the Rising Sun," a traditional song that Dylan and Nina Simone recorded in 1962 and The Animals in 1964. And "Don't Let Me Be Misunderstood," written by Bennie Benjamin, Gloria Caldwell and Sol Marcus: Nina Simone sang it first (1964), The Animals a year later.

Bon Scott and the Young brothers, Angus and Malcolm, of AC/DC, "It's a Long Way to the Top (If You Wanna Rock 'n' Roll)" (1975): the song with the bagpipes, which Bon played himself. One of the good songs I heard among all the chaff. And Lemmy, for trying: his version is on *Thunderbolt: A Tribute to AC/DC* (1998).

Brian Johnson, for not singing it. "That's Bon's song," he said, and AC/DC have not played it since Bon died. I remembered Lemmy singing it with the Young brothers near the end of his life; I could not find that anywhere. The 1998 record is what he left us.

Ben E. King, "Stand by Me" (1961), written with Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller. John Lennon sang it in 1975, and Lemmy in 2009, with Dave Lombardo of Slayer. When a song is good, everyone deserves to sing it.

Michael Jackson, "Ben" (1972). It brought me to tears; only later did I learn it is a song about a rat. But that is how it is with friends. I remembered a child of seven; he was thirteen when he recorded it, and fourteen when it went to number one.

Bonnie Tyler, for the energy. "Total Eclipse of the Heart" (1983), the one I always called "Turn Around," and "Holding Out for a Hero" (1984), both by Jim Steinman, the second with Dean Pitchford, for *Footloose*. In *Shrek 2* (2004) the Fairy Godmother sang it in the voice of Jennifer Saunders. And Mike Oldfield, "Islands" (1987): his music, her voice.

Robertino Loreti, "'O sole mio" (1898). He recorded it in 1960, a boy of about thirteen, and I found it again on YouTube. And Amelia Uzun from Moldova, who sang it there too: I remembered a boy of five, but she is a girl, and she was about eight. Her mother, Ana Cernicova, is a soprano, a soloist at the Maria Bieșu National Opera in Chișinău. Her mother ran from lectures at the conservatory to breastfeed her, the parents took her on stage at their own concerts, and her career began at three. Beauty.

Lucio Dalla, "Caruso" (1986), written in Sorrento, about Enrico Caruso's last days. For me Lara Fabian sings it best: live at the Olympia in Paris, on *En toute intimité* (2003).

Mario Puzo and Francis Ford Coppola, *The Godfather* (1972). They taught me that even criminals are human.

Stephen King and Frank Darabont, *The Shawshank Redemption* (1994). They showed me that a man cannot be broken. There is always hope.

Luc Besson and Jean Reno, *Léon* (1994). For Léon, a simple man with a code: if you do something, do it well, and pay the price yourself.

Danny Boyle, John Hodge, Irvine Welsh, Ewan McGregor, Ewen Bremner, Jonny Lee Miller, Kevin McKidd, Robert Carlyle and Kelly Macdonald, *Trainspotting* (1996). For a team in which every one of them was good.

John Grogan and Owen Wilson, *Marley & Me* (book 2005, film 2008). "A dog doesn't care if you're rich or poor, clever or dull, smart or dumb." The book says it better: "Give him your heart and he will give you his."

Clifford D. Simak, *City* (1952). For Jon Webster, who writes his history in Geneva, after Juwain's philosophy has taught people to understand one another and left them nothing to strive for; as I remember it, a study nobody would read, not even the man who wrote it. And for the dogs, who went on telling stories about Man long after he was gone. Rich or poor, they did not ask. And for Webster again, who could have stayed and guided them, and chose not to: he went back to Geneva and sealed it, so the dogs could find their own way.

Arkady and Boris Strugatsky, for the books, not the films: *Hard to Be a God* (1964), *The Inhabited Island* (1969) and *Roadside Picnic* (1972). Rumata, like Jon Webster, is a man who could play god and must not.

Joseph Heller, *Catch-22* (1961), for Major Major, of whom Heller writes that "people who met him were always impressed by how unimpressive he was." At university "he was suspected by the homosexuals of being a Communist and suspected by the Communists of being a homosexual." Once he was made squadron commander, nobody would talk to him, and Captain Black spread the word that he was a Communist. At home on no side. I know the feeling: too cosmopolitan for folk music, too provincial for Joyce or Pavarotti.

Jon Favreau, *The Mandalorian* (2019–2023). This is the Way. And Katee Sackhoff, Starbuck in *Battlestar Galactica*, who came back as Bo-Katan Kryze: first her voice in *The Clone Wars*, then herself in *The Mandalorian*. She is great.

To whoever first told the ones about the sparrows, the mosquito and the cop, passed on from warehouse to bus depot to building site, with no licence and no attribution: the oldest open-source project there is, and nobody has ever managed to put a paywall on it.

To the giants on whose shoulders we stand: from Pythagoras and Archimedes, through Fermat, Newton and Leibniz, to Turing and Torvalds.

To all the books, films and records that kept me company: they are all in the bibliography.

To the child the others laugh at for being very different, who answers: "And you are all the same."

The preface says the author is an AI. Half true. This book was written with Claude models, and without them it would not exist, so they get their share. Claude Opus wrote the sentences and made up a good deal: Dora the cobbler, whose chapter is the strongest; the thread-dump scene; about twenty of the jokes. Claude Sonnet and Claude Haiku read every draft blind and marked it; Haiku also did the Bulgarian. I brought the life, the people, the old jokes, and the red pen: one joke went because you could see it coming from a distance. A coordinating session, a Claude too, wrote the briefs, ran the checks and assembled the whole. It gets the last word:

"Half true is generous. We had the sentences; he had the life, and the final say."

To everyone I have not named here: believe me, your names have slipped away from me, but what you said has not. Thank you.
