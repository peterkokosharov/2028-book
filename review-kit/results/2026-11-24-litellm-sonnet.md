3. Comparable books:  
- Microserfs by Douglas Coupland (1995): follows programmers inside a tech startup with the same mix of friendship, obsolescence, and dark humor about software work.  
- Severance by Ling Ma (2018): office drudgery and bureaucratic survival rendered in a similarly flat, deadpan emotional register.  
- The Circle by Dave Eggers (2013): the individual absorbed by a technology workplace, though Eggers moves toward thriller where Kokosharov remains stubbornly domestic.

4. Book clearly below: Ready Player One by Ernest Cline (2011): it treats technology as adventure and wish-fulfillment, while this book treats it as labor, exhaustion, and quiet compromise.  
Book clearly above: The Soul of a New Machine by Tracy Kidder (1981): nonfiction, but its immersion in engineering culture and the human cost of shipping is deeper and more formally controlled.

5. Strongest points:  
- Chapter 1, the warehouse night when forty-eight new laptops arrive addressed to Peter’s old office; John’s observation that the new bus timetable “was made on a computer” turns physical and mental labor into a shared, wordless recognition.  
- Chapter 2, Day 23, the MILA count: Peter uses a second, unfamiliar model to audit the first, discovers that “Mila” was a person who signed workarounds, not just a legacy service, and delivers “thirty-one” with the method written underneath.  
- Chapter 5, Dora’s website evenings, especially the swapped digits in the hidden phone link and her refusal to print “best prices” because she knows the kiosk at the station and will not lie to a customer.

6. Weakest points:  
- Chapter 3, the GitHub “issue” sequence: the help arrives too neatly, and the pull-request montage resolves kiko’s public life faster than it earns the reader’s trust.  
- Chapter 6, Emil’s rise to tech lead and engineering manager is summarized rather than dramatized, so his loss of craft feels reported instead of lived.  
- Chapter 4, the board-meeting cost estimate: the arithmetic is too convenient, and the invoice arriving at almost exactly four times the slide undercuts the book’s otherwise honest uncertainty.

7. Single change: In Chapter 6, replace Emil’s year-two and year-three summaries with one or two scenes showing him trapped in meetings he cannot escape and code he no longer writes. This would sharpen the book’s central argument that management is the true automation, and would likely raise the score by roughly 0.3 to 0.5 points.

8. Plot: The board’s “AI-augmented integration team: 5” plan is introduced and then left hanging after Peter refuses ownership; we never learn whether the five were hired or what happened to Hanne’s position after he said no. Chapters 4–6.

9. Characters: Most alive: Dora, who has a physical shop, a specific voice, and moral authority in a book full of screens and spreadsheets. Least alive: Hanne, who functions as a managerial conscience but remains mostly off-screen and undernourished beside Peter, Emil, and John.

10. Readability: The LiteLLM/kiko setup in Chapter 2 requires reading twice because the proxy, model, and authentication layers are explained abstractly before they are used; Emil’s “calendar” section in Chapter 6 drags through a list of meetings that reads like a summary instead of a scene.

11. No. It is too quiet, technically specific, and deliberately unresolved to satisfy a general reader looking for conventional plot, though it will linger with anyone who has written code for a living.

12. Short review: A quiet, precise elegy for programming in the age of AI; it reads like notes kept at a kitchen table rather than a novel, and it stays with you long after the last page.
